A browser simulation of the proposed system: four water sensors, a servo feeder with an IR beam-break check, and the ask-then-fallback feeding logic. Nothing here comes from hardware, and every value is demo data.

Upgrades beyond the brief

SIM

These go beyond the original brief. The group has confirmed the three-stage filter design. The controller, arm and camera are proposals that need project-team confirmation. None has been built or tested, and every value shown for them is simulation / demo data.

  • Universal controller. A fixed 24 × 7 × 4.5 cm housing rests on the front rim. Rails slide out of both ends to corner clamps, spanning 31.4–49.9 cm wide and 15.7–24.9 cm deep across the six tank settings.
  • Mini 4-axis feeder arm. Turntable, shoulder, elbow and wrist, with a reach of about 8.7 cm from the housing centre. An IR gate on the scoop lip confirms the drop, and no passage is a fault. The tank button still decides whether to feed, with the same cap of 3 a day. The camera only picks where to pour.
  • Top camera and blind spots. One fixed 120° lens sees about 81° under water. It covers about 100 % of the floor but only about 3–8 % of the water just under the surface, so the upper ends and sides are blind. The behaviour analysis is a rule-based stand-in, not a trained model.
  • Three-stage filter (team-confirmed design). Off by default. A pump loop with a sponge, biological media and optional activated carbon. It warns below 60 % of set flow, makes a protective stop below 30 % until the sponge is cleaned, and stops on low water. It never regulates pH or TDS. All constants are illustrative, to confirm by measurement.

          How it works in this simulation

          • One controller for every tank. The head, display, hopper, arm and camera are the same size on all six tank settings. Two telescoping rails and four clamps stretch to the tank that the phone reports. The camera keeps one fixed 120° lens on every size, about 81° under water, so it has blind spots near the surface.
          • Analysis is a rule-based stand-in. It reads the simulated fish positions: how fast they move compared with this tank's own normal (learned in the first seconds), how many are near the surface, and how many come to the food after a feed. A real build would need a trained detector on camera frames. None exists or has been tested here. The blind-spot count comes from the simulation; a real camera cannot know what it does not see. Hiding behind plants or rocks is not modelled.
          • The camera picks where to drop food, not whether. When a feed is approved with the tank button, the mini arm carries the scoop over the spot where the fish are, inside its reach. The pellets then fall, float and sink under simple physics. Proposed upgrade; needs project-team confirmation.
          • The rule limits are placeholders. The project team has not confirmed any alert limit, so alerts are off until you switch them on, and every line is labelled as a demo rule.

                  What the paper reports (its results, not ours)

                  • Sensor accuracy over 200 trials: temperature 96.5 %, turbidity 92.5 %, pH 91 %, TDS 88 %.
                  • The abstract states 96 % average accuracy, but these four table figures average 92.0 %. Cite the table, and point out the mismatch.
                  • Test setting: 10-litre tank, five goldfish, 72-hour operation. Controlled changes: a heater raised temperature, vinegar lowered pH, soil raised turbidity.
                  • Response timing: 1.8–2.3 s in the parameter table, and 1.2 s anomaly response in the abstract.
                  • Limitations: pH drift above 8.0 needs recalibration. TDS accuracy falls at high range. Bubbles affect turbidity. The system depends on Wi-Fi. The validation was small-scale.

                  This demo copies the method (sensor set, processing rule, perturbation tests) so it can be shown. None of the figures above are produced or confirmed by this simulation.

                  Why ask, then fall back?

                  Reviewed paper [3] compared manual and automatic feeding. AquaCare proposes a third option: ask the owner first, and feed automatically only if nobody presses the tank button within 10 minutes.

                  This tool runs the same 30 days three times, once per mode. The seed, simulated owner, feed times and scheduled faults are identical; only the feeding mode changes.

                  What this compares: feeding logic under a simulated owner who answers a set share of reminders. It does not model fish growth, stress, survival or water cloudiness. Those outcomes appear below only as paper [3]'s reported results.

                        Calibration status: Not calibrated. The simulated pH sensor has no calibration data. The pH values on this page are generated numbers, not calibrated measurements.

                        Why calibration is required

                        An analog pH probe's output drifts with age, temperature and storage. The ±0.1 pH target cannot be claimed until the real probe has been calibrated and then checked against a reference.

                        General procedure for the real sensor

                        1. Follow the calibration method in the chosen sensor module's datasheet. It takes priority over this outline.
                        2. Rinse the probe with distilled water and blot it dry between solutions.
                        3. Place it in the first standard buffer solution, let the reading settle, and record the raw value.
                        4. Repeat with at least one more buffer to set the slope (two-point or three-point calibration).
                        5. Store the calibration constants in the controller and note the date.
                        6. Verify against a buffer not used for calibration before trusting readings.
